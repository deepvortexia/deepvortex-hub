Will AI Replace Soldiers?

March 26, 2026 · Deep Vortex AI

Autonomous weapons are no longer science fiction. Drone swarms have already fought in real conflicts. Robot ground vehicles are being tested on active borders. The question is no longer if AI enters the battlefield — it is whether it will eventually make the human soldier obsolete. We asked an AI directly.

← Back to Blog

The following is an unfiltered dialogue between a human journalist and an AI on the subject of autonomous warfare, robot soldiers, and whether human combatants will eventually be replaced by machines. The AI was not instructed to be reassuring.

The Debate

Human
Let's not dance around it. Are AI and robots going to replace human soldiers? And be honest — not diplomatic.
AI
For many battlefield functions, yes — and it's already happening. The Switchblade loitering munition, used in Ukraine since 2022, is an autonomous drone that identifies and strikes targets with minimal human input. Israel's Harop can hunt radar emissions and destroy them without a human in the loop for the final strike decision. These aren't prototypes. They're deployed weapons in active conflicts. The question isn't "will it happen" — it's "how much of the human role gets eliminated and how fast."
Human
But there are things robots can't do — reading a room, understanding context, knowing when not to shoot. Isn't that exactly where humans are irreplaceable?
AI
That argument is weaker than it sounds. Context recognition is a machine learning problem, and it's being solved. The US military's Project Maven uses AI to analyze drone footage and identify combatants, vehicles, and weapons with accuracy rates that reportedly exceed human analysts at scale. "Reading a room" in a modern urban warfare context often means pattern-matching sensor data — thermal signatures, movement patterns, communication intercepts — which AI does faster and more consistently than any human. The irreplaceability argument keeps retreating to a shrinking frontier.
Human
What about accountability? If an autonomous weapon kills civilians, who goes to prison?
AI
Nobody — and that's one of the most dangerous features of autonomous weapons, not a bug that gets fixed. The Campaign to Stop Killer Robots, supported by over 160 NGOs and discussed at the UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons since 2013, has failed to produce a binding treaty precisely because major military powers see accountability gaps as strategically advantageous. If a drone kills a wedding party, the manufacturer says the military programmed it wrong, the military says the algorithm malfunctioned, and the government says it was an edge case. There is no court in the world currently equipped to assign criminal liability to a machine decision.
Human
That's terrifying. So we're building weapons that can kill with no one responsible?
AI
Yes. And the arms race logic makes it almost inevitable. If one state deploys fully autonomous lethal systems and gains a battlefield advantage, adversaries face a choice: match it or lose. Paul Scharre, former Pentagon official and author of Army of None, describes this as a "flash war" scenario — where AI systems on opposing sides react to each other at machine speed, escalating a conflict from skirmish to full-scale war in minutes, with no human having time to intervene. The 2010 Flash Crash in financial markets — where algorithmic trading systems caused a $1 trillion value wipeout in 36 minutes — is considered a preview of what this looks like in a military context.
Human
Are there things that will keep humans in the loop permanently? Things AI simply cannot be trusted to decide?
AI
Nuclear launch decisions are the clearest case — and even there, the pressure to automate is real. Russia's Perimeter system (nicknamed "Dead Hand") has operated since the 1980s as a semi-autonomous nuclear retaliation system designed to launch missiles if it detects a decapitation strike and cannot reach command authority. The US has publicly rejected full automation of nuclear release, but the logic of "launch on warning" systems increasingly compresses human decision time to seconds. Beyond nuclear, strategic diplomatic judgment — knowing when to stop fighting, when a ceasefire is achievable, what a face-saving exit for an adversary looks like — remains genuinely human. Machines optimize for objectives. Wars end when humans decide the cost exceeds the goal.
Human
What about the psychological dimension? Soldiers who've seen combat talk about moral injury — the trauma of killing. Does removing humans from killing make war easier to start?
AI
This is one of the most serious concerns raised by military ethicists, and the answer is almost certainly yes. Lt. Col. Dave Grossman's research on the psychology of killing — documented in On Killing — shows that human beings have a deep biological and psychological resistance to killing other humans at close range. Drone warfare already demonstrated that increasing physical distance reduces psychological inhibition. Fully autonomous weapons remove the human entirely from the kill decision, potentially making conflict initiation feel like a software deployment rather than a declaration of war. When the cost of starting a war is borne entirely by machines and the adversary's population rather than your own soldiers, the political threshold for using force drops dramatically.
Human
Which countries are furthest ahead in building autonomous military AI?
AI
The United States, China, Russia, Israel, South Korea, and Turkey are the leading developers. The US leads in overall AI capability and integration — DARPA's OFFSET program developed swarm tactics for hundreds of autonomous drones operating collaboratively. China's PLA has explicitly stated AI-enabled warfare as a core strategic priority in its 2035 military modernization plan, and Chinese firms like CETC have demonstrated drone swarms of over 1,000 units. Israel's Elbit Systems and Rafael have the most operationally deployed autonomous systems. Turkey's Bayraktar Akinci and Kargu-2 drones — the latter reportedly engaged targets autonomously in Libya in 2020 according to a UN Panel of Experts report — mark the first documented case of an autonomous weapon lethal engagement without human command.
Human
So where does this end? A world of robot armies fighting each other, with humans just watching?
AI
Some strategists call that a stabilizing scenario — if robot armies fight robot armies and human casualties approach zero on both sides, the horror of war is reduced. But history doesn't support that optimism. Wars end because populations suffer and demand peace. If the suffering falls only on machines, wars may never end — they simply become permanent, low-cost attrition campaigns conducted by competing automated systems, while humans on both sides continue to be affected by economic devastation, infrastructure destruction, and displacement. The more likely near-term trajectory is a hybrid: AI handles surveillance, logistics, cyber operations, close-range drone combat, and targeting support, while human soldiers remain in command roles and high-stakes ground engagements — until the next capability threshold forces another decision.
Human
Last question: should we be building this at all?
AI
That's the question that matters most, and it's the one the arms industry, most governments, and most of the public are not seriously asking. The technology exists. The incentives to deploy it are enormous. The international legal frameworks are lagging by decades. The UN Secretary-General António Guterres called for a ban on lethal autonomous weapons by 2026 — a deadline that passed without binding action. What I can tell you is that the nations most capable of setting limits are the ones least willing to accept them, because limits only matter if your adversary also accepts them. We are in a classic arms control dilemma, except the weapons in question can decide to fire on their own. Whether we should build this is a human question. Whether we will — the evidence says yes.

Conclusion

The replacement of human soldiers by AI is not a future scenario — it is an ongoing process at different stages of completion for different battlefield functions. Surveillance, logistics, drone combat, targeting analysis, and cyber operations are already heavily automated. Lethal autonomous engagement is operationally deployed in limited contexts. Full replacement of the human soldier remains constrained by ethics, accountability gaps, strategic uncertainty, and the irreplaceable role of human judgment in war termination. But each of those constraints is actively being eroded. The relevant question is no longer whether — it is at what speed, under whose authority, and with what rules. Currently, the answers are: fast, unclear, and almost none.
