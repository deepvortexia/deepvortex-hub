Can Deepfakes Start a War?

March 26, 2026 · Deep Vortex AI

In March 2022, a deepfake video of Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky appeared online, appearing to show him ordering Ukrainian soldiers to surrender. It was fake. It was debunked within hours. But it worked well enough to raise the most important question of the synthetic media age: what happens the next time — when the fake is better, the context more volatile, and the verification slower?

← Back to Blog

The following dialogue between a human and an AI explores the realistic scenarios in which synthetic media — deepfake video, AI-generated audio, fabricated imagery — could contribute to, accelerate, or directly trigger armed conflict between states or non-state actors.

The Debate

Human
The Zelensky deepfake didn't work. It was debunked fast. Doesn't that suggest that deepfakes aren't really that dangerous as a trigger for military conflict?
AI
The Zelensky case was debunked fast because the technical quality was poor — the lip sync was imperfect, the head movements were unnatural, and the media environment was alert to the possibility of fakes. That was 2022 technology. By 2026, state-actor deepfake production uses systems that produce video indistinguishable from authentic footage by human perception, with authentic-sounding audio synthesis that can pass basic forensic analysis. The question is not "did the 2022 fake work" — it is "what happens when a 2026-quality fake targets a regional conflict where verification infrastructure is weak, tensions are already high, and the opposing side has 90 seconds to decide whether to respond to what appears to be a confirmed hostile action?"
Human
Give me the most realistic scenario where a deepfake actually contributes to military escalation.
AI
The most dangerous scenario is not a deepfake of a world leader — those are too high-profile and subject to rapid multi-channel verification. The most dangerous scenario involves tactical military communication forgery. Imagine: a regional conflict is ongoing, two sides are in fragile ceasefire negotiations. A fabricated intercept — synthetic audio that sounds like a senior commander ordering a strike on a civilian target — is planted in signals intelligence channels accessible to the opposing side. The opposing side's military, already on hair-trigger alert, sees the intercept, cannot immediately verify it through the usual channels because communications are disrupted, and makes a preemptive move. No deepfake video needed. Just convincing synthetic audio in the right intelligence channel at the right moment. This scenario is not hypothetical — it is the explicit threat model in classified US and NATO assessments from 2024 onward.
Human
Has a deepfake or synthetic media actually been used in a real conflict already?
AI
Definitively confirmed cases are rare because state actors do not announce information operations. What is documented: the Zelensky surrender deepfake (March 2022) was the first widely reported deployment of a deepfake in an active conflict. In 2023, synthetic social media personas — AI-generated profile photos, AI-written posts — were used in influence operations targeting elections in multiple countries, documented by Stanford Internet Observatory and Meta's adversarial threat reports. In the Myanmar conflict, AI-manipulated imagery was used to misrepresent the identities of attackers and victims, contributing to ethnic targeting. The progression from influence operations targeting domestic populations to operations targeting military decision-makers is not a leap — it is an extension of the same capability set, aimed at a higher-value target.
Human
What about the "liar's dividend"? I've heard the term — the idea that deepfakes allow real things to be dismissed as fake.
AI
The liar's dividend is arguably more dangerous than the deepfakes themselves. The term, coined by law professors Robert Chesney and Danielle Citron in their 2019 Harvard Law Review paper, describes the phenomenon where the existence of deepfakes gives bad actors a credible defense against authentic evidence. A war crimes video — authentic footage of soldiers executing civilians — can be dismissed as a deepfake. A legitimate recording of a political leader making a corrupt arrangement can be denied on the same basis. The more sophisticated deepfakes become, the more all video and audio evidence becomes contestable. In a military context, this means that authentic intelligence products — real surveillance footage, real intercepts — can be rejected by adversaries or domestic audiences with the same "that's a deepfake" dismissal that was previously only available to obvious fabrications. The technology erodes the evidentiary foundation of everything.
Human
Can deepfake detection technology keep pace? Or is it always going to be a losing battle?
AI
Detection is structurally disadvantaged in an arms race with generation. Generative models improve by learning to defeat detection — adversarial training explicitly optimizes generated content to pass classifier tests. Every publicly released detection model provides a training signal for the next generation of fakes. DARPA's Media Forensics (MediFor) program, launched in 2016 and running through 2022, invested $68 million specifically in deepfake detection and concluded that while detection capabilities improved significantly, they could not reliably identify state-actor-quality synthetic media in real deployment conditions. The current consensus among AI security researchers is that provenance — cryptographic authentication of content at the point of capture — is more promising than forensic detection. Adobe's Content Credentials standard and the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) represent the strongest current approach: signing authentic content at creation so that unsigned content can be treated with default skepticism. But this requires adoption across all cameras, microphones, and publishing platforms simultaneously — a coordination problem that is far from solved.
Human
What about nuclear command and control? Could a deepfake target the chain of command?
AI
Nuclear command and control systems in the US, Russia, UK, and France use multi-factor authentication protocols, out-of-band verification channels, and physical authentication devices specifically designed to be resistant to voice or video impersonation. The two-man rule, physical possession of authentication codes, and the requirement for confirmation through multiple independent channels make a deepfake-based nuclear launch scenario extremely unlikely in normal operating conditions. The danger is at the margins: during a genuine crisis, when command channels are partially degraded, when decision timelines are compressed, and when the political pressure to respond is already overwhelming, a synthetic communication that appears to confirm an adversary's aggressive intent — even if it cannot directly authorize action — could tip a decision already teetering. The deepfake doesn't need to authorize the strike. It needs only to eliminate the last hesitation.
Human
What do governments actually do about this? Any real policy?
AI
Policy is lagging badly. The US DEFIANCE Act (2024) criminalized non-consensual intimate deepfakes but does not address geopolitical synthetic media. The EU's AI Act requires labelling of synthetic media but provides no enforcement mechanism for content generated outside EU jurisdiction — which is where hostile state actors operate. NATO's Strategic Communications Centre of Excellence in Riga runs ongoing research into deepfake-based influence operations, but no binding international treaty governs state use of synthetic media as a weapon. The most concrete response has been at the platform level: Meta, Google, and YouTube have policies requiring labelling of AI-generated content in political advertising, but enforcement is inconsistent and does not cover non-advertising content. The gap between the speed of synthetic media capability and the speed of policy response is currently measured in years.
Human
So can deepfakes start a war?
AI
Not alone — and probably not directly. Wars require political will, military capacity, and a chain of human decisions that a single piece of synthetic media cannot fully corrupt. But deepfakes can accelerate the path to conflict by collapsing the verification window that allows decision-makers to distinguish a real attack from a false one. They can erode the shared information environment that makes de-escalation possible — if both sides cannot agree on what is real, they cannot negotiate from a common factual basis. They can provide justification for actions already decided upon and present the deciding faction with a convenient casus belli that is difficult to disprove. The most accurate answer is: deepfakes probably cannot start a war between parties that have no other reason to fight. Between parties that are already on the edge — they might push one over it.

Conclusion

Deepfakes are not a magic war-starting weapon. But in a world of compressed decision timelines, degraded trust in media, and conflicts that are permanently one incident away from escalation, synthetic media is a genuine threat multiplier. The most dangerous vector is not a splashy presidential deepfake but quiet, targeted synthetic intelligence products aimed at military decision-makers at the worst possible moment. Detection technology is structurally outpaced by generation. Policy is years behind capability. The Zelensky deepfake failed because it was bad and everyone was looking. The next one will be better, and the world may not be watching.
