Can AI Predict the Next War?

March 26, 2026 · Deep Vortex AI

Palantir is running conflict prediction models for the US military. The UN is using machine learning to anticipate humanitarian crises. Academic projects like ViEWS and ACLED feed satellite data, social media sentiment, and economic indicators into algorithms designed to forecast political violence months in advance. The question: does any of it actually work — and is a world where AI predicts wars more stable, or more dangerous?

The following is a dialogue between a human analyst and an AI on the science, politics, and deeply uncomfortable implications of using artificial intelligence to predict armed conflict before it starts.

The Debate

Human
Can AI actually predict wars? Or is this just another case of tech overpromising on something inherently unpredictable?
AI
The honest answer is: better than chance, worse than advertised, and useful for the right questions. AI systems trained on historical conflict data — economic indicators, political instability indices, military mobilization patterns, social media sentiment, satellite imagery analysis — can identify risk elevation in specific regions with statistical accuracy that exceeds human analyst baselines on certain forecasting horizons. The ViEWS (Violence & Impacts Early Warning System), developed for the UN, published peer-reviewed accuracy assessments showing meaningful predictive power for political violence at the subnational level 6 months ahead. The ACLED (Armed Conflict Location and Event Data) project feeds real-time conflict event data into models used by governments, NGOs, and military planners across four continents. These are real capabilities. They are not crystal balls — but they are a better baseline than unaided expert intuition on most forecasting tasks.
Human
The 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine — did any AI system predict it?
AI
Several systems flagged elevated conflict risk. Satellite imagery analysis — commercially available from Planet Labs and Maxar — showed the Russian military buildup along the Ukrainian border from October 2021 onward with high specificity. US intelligence, using classified AI-assisted analysis of signals intelligence and imagery, reportedly assessed with high confidence that an invasion was being prepared as early as December 2021. What AI could not determine — and what human analysts also failed to resolve — was Putin's intent: whether the buildup was genuine preparation for invasion or a coercive bluff designed to extract political concessions. AI is good at detecting capability. It is significantly weaker at detecting intent, which is a function of psychology, internal political dynamics, and individual decision-making that does not leave reliable signatures in the data streams AI systems monitor.
Human
What data do these systems actually use? What are they looking at?
AI
The input streams vary by system, but the most capable ones integrate: satellite and aerial imagery for military movement, infrastructure construction, and population displacement; economic data — commodity prices, currency stability, trade volumes, which historically correlate with political instability; political event data — election results, government composition changes, coup attempts, protest events catalogued by systems like GDELT (Global Database of Events, Language, and Tone) which processes news from 65 languages in real time; social media sentiment analysis in local languages as an early indicator of social tension; demographic and climate data — food security, water stress, population density in contested border areas; and historical conflict data going back decades, which provides the base rate distributions the models are calibrated against. The most sophisticated systems use graph neural networks to model the relationships between state and non-state actors rather than treating them as independent variables.
Human
Here's what worries me: if an AI tells a government "70% probability of conflict with country X in the next 6 months," doesn't that prediction itself influence behavior? Couldn't it trigger a preemptive strike?
AI
You've identified the most serious and least-discussed problem in conflict prediction AI — the self-fulfilling prophecy problem. If both sides in a rivalry have AI systems predicting high conflict probability, and both sides use that prediction to justify defensive preparations, those preparations are observable by the other side's AI systems, which update their predictions upward, which justifies further preparations. This is a prediction-reaction spiral that can produce conflict not despite the forecasting systems but because of them. The analogy to the 1914 mobilization dynamics is not coincidental — the Austro-Hungarian, German, Russian, and French general staff mobilization timetables were so tightly interlocked that each side's defensive mobilization looked like offensive preparation to the other, and the process became self-reinforcing. AI conflict prediction at military speed, feeding into AI-assisted decision systems, could reproduce that dynamic in days rather than weeks. This is taken seriously enough that it appears in the US National Security Commission on AI's 2021 report as a primary stability concern.
Human
What about predicting non-state conflicts — insurgencies, civil wars, terrorism? Is AI better at those?
AI
For subnational violence, AI predictive systems have demonstrated stronger and more consistently validated accuracy. The reasons are structural: civil conflicts and insurgencies have longer buildup periods and leave more detectable signatures in the data streams AI monitors — economic marginalization, governance failures, ethnic tension indices, displacement patterns — before violence erupts. The Early Warning Project, run by the US Holocaust Memorial Museum, uses machine learning to generate ranked lists of countries at highest risk for mass atrocity events. Its retrospective analysis shows that it would have flagged most major mass atrocity events in the post-Cold War period well in advance. The UNHCR uses predictive models to pre-position humanitarian resources before displacement crises peak. The Iridis system, used by USAID, integrates 60+ data streams to forecast food security crises with 3-6 month lead times. These are genuinely useful applications where the prediction does not itself create the predicted event.
Human
What's the worst failure mode? Where could an AI war-prediction system cause the most damage?
AI
The worst failure mode is a false positive acted upon by an automated response system. If an AI conflict prediction system is integrated with an automated military response framework — pre-positioned assets, triggered alerts, automatic diplomatic communications — a high-confidence false prediction of imminent attack could initiate a sequence of defensive actions that the adversary's AI systems interpret as offensive preparations, triggering their own response sequence. This is sometimes called the "hair trigger" problem. A related failure mode is adversarial manipulation: if a state knows that an adversary's conflict prediction AI is monitoring specific indicators — troop movements, satellite communications, social media patterns — it can deliberately generate false signals to manipulate the prediction output, causing the adversary to misallocate defensive resources or, worse, make preemptive moves based on fabricated threat indicators. This is the AI equivalent of the classic military deception operation, updated for the age of machine learning.
Human
Is there anything AI genuinely cannot predict about war?
AI
Several things. The decisions of individual leaders under extreme pressure — what Stalin chose to believe about the German buildup in June 1941 despite overwhelming intelligence of an imminent invasion, what drove Saddam Hussein's actual calculus before the Gulf War — involve psychology, ego, misinformation within the leader's own intelligence apparatus, and genuine irrationality that does not produce clean data signatures. Black swan events — the assassination of Franz Ferdinand, the self-immolation of Mohamed Bouazizi that triggered the Arab Spring — are by definition outside the training distribution of any historical model. Technological disruptions that change the fundamental logic of conflict — like the introduction of nuclear weapons in 1945 — cannot be predicted from pre-disruption data. And perhaps most importantly, the choices of ordinary people — soldiers who refuse to fire, populations that resist rather than capitulate, civilians who choose collective action at decisive moments — are the most poorly modeled inputs in any quantitative conflict prediction framework. Human courage and cowardice at scale are not reliably predictable by machine.
Human
Bottom line: should governments be using AI to predict wars?
AI
For early warning applications — humanitarian pre-positioning, diplomatic early engagement, resource allocation for conflict prevention — yes, with strong human oversight and with the explicit understanding that predictions are probabilistic inputs to human judgment, not mandates for action. For military decision-making systems where the prediction feeds into automated response frameworks — the answer requires extreme caution and governance frameworks that do not yet exist. The difference between "this region has elevated conflict risk, let's increase diplomatic engagement" and "this adversary is at 70% attack probability, initiate defensive posture Alpha" is the difference between a decision support tool and a decision replacement system. The first is valuable. The second is dangerous. The problem is that the same underlying technology enables both, and the institutions governing its use are not moving fast enough to maintain that distinction under the operational pressures of a real crisis.

Conclusion

AI can predict conditions that make war more likely — economic collapse, military mobilization, governance breakdown, social fragmentation — with accuracy that genuinely exceeds unaided human forecasting on certain horizons. It cannot reliably predict the intent of individual leaders, black swan triggers, or the cumulative choices of populations under pressure. The most valuable applications are in humanitarian early warning and conflict prevention, where predictions inform human diplomacy without creating the self-fulfilling prophecy dynamics that make military prediction systems dangerous. The most dangerous scenario is AI prediction integrated with automated military response, potentially producing escalation spirals that move faster than human decision-making can interrupt. The technology exists. The governance does not.
